<Section id="research" eyebrow="Research" title="Going to the construction site">

<Callout>
  Our users were busy construction professionals who needed lots of notice to join a session. So **we went to them**: on-site at construction sites, with our equipment, asking contractors to talk through the checkout in their own words.
</Callout>

Out of twelve interviews, **seven users expressed frustration with checkout**. We also interviewed CSRs, who heard customer complaints every day, and reviewed competitors including Reno Run and Toolbx.

<Section id="design" eyebrow="Design" title="Working within the project model" status="Shipped">

Biiibo's backend required users to create a "Project" with an address before ordering, a quirk that shaped the original confusing checkout. **The challenge was to keep that requirement while making it feel natural.**

<Figure
  image="Address select (formerly Projects), before and after"
  caption="Projects became a simple address choice."
  pins={[{ x: 50, y: 30 }]}
  notes={[
    { title: 'Address select', text: '"Projects" were reframed as choosing a delivery address, which contractors already understand.' },
  ]}
/>

<Figure
  image="Redesigned delivery options + order review"
  caption="Delivery options with prices, and a clearer order review."
  ratio="16 / 9"
  pins={[{ x: 30, y: 50 }, { x: 72, y: 50 }]}
  notes={[
    { title: 'Priced delivery options', text: 'Contractors told us they needed prices to compare options, so every option shows one.' },
    { title: 'Separate address and card steps', text: 'Testing showed that adding an address and a card on one screen felt cluttered, so they became separate pages.' },
  ]}
/>

I tested low-fidelity wireframes with users and the CSR team, then moved to high fidelity. Many participants found it hard to react to wireframes, so **high-fidelity prototypes were essential for this audience.**

</Section>
